HabitTune: AI-Generated Personalized Children’s Songs for Positive Habit Development

Personalization Children’s songs Habit formation

Background

Everyday habits such as toothbrushing, handwashing, and tidying can become moments of resistance and repeated reminders. Children's songs offer rhythm and repetition, but generic songs may not reflect each child's interests or reasons for avoiding an activity.

HabitTune explores how generative AI can make these routines more personal and enjoyable. It creates personalized songs that acknowledge children's reluctance and emphasize positive, concrete benefits, encouraging participation through playful support.

How It Works

  • Choose a target habit. A parent selects a daily activity and enters the child's reason for resisting it through voice or text.
  • Personalize the lyrics. The child's name and favorite foods, colors, animals, or characters shape supportive lyrics that acknowledge reluctance and frame the activity positively.
  • Create the song together. Parents and children choose an instrument, rhythm, and mood. The app combines these preferences with the lyrics to generate a song for use before or during the routine.

User Study

We conducted a two-week home study with families of children aged 3–6, analyzing nine families after one withdrawal. Parents and children co-created a song, and parents were asked to play it at least twice daily and submit daily reports.

Playback logs, pre- and post-study questionnaires, and parent interviews captured how songs fit into family routines. Parents rated the app as highly usable, with a mean System Usability Scale score of 82.75 out of 100.

What We Learned

Families often played songs immediately before or during the target activity. Parents described children singing along, responding to familiar details, and treating routines as play or a mission. Some reported easier transitions, less resistance, and fewer repeated verbal reminders.

Some children also performed the activity without the song, while others lost interest over time. These parent-reported patterns suggest potential for supporting everyday routines, but the short study does not establish lasting habit formation. Musical variation and songs matched to routine length are promising directions for further study.
