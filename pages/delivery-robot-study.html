Understanding Human–Robot Interaction in Commercial Delivery Services

I am a co-first author of this paper, which is currently under review. (The title and teaser figure shown here have been slightly modified from the submitted version to preserve anonymity during review.)

Delivery robots Service interactions Field research

Overview

We study how people encounter and interact with robots in an everyday commercial delivery service. Instead of centering the robot's autonomy, we treat a delivery as something several people take part in: the customer waiting for an order, the passers-by who share the street with the robot, and the remote staff who watch over each trip. We focus on what happens between the robot's arrival and a completed delivery, and on what interaction design can do to support it.

Background

Delivery robots now move through public spaces where people differ in their needs, expectations, and familiarity with robots. Reaching the delivery location is only part of the job. The customer still has to notice the robot, walk out to it, work out how to open it, and take out their items, often while other people and vehicles are moving nearby.

These moments are easy to overlook in discussions of autonomous navigation, yet they are where a delivery actually succeeds or stalls. Looking closely at such ordinary encounters can inform services that feel clearer and more approachable to everyone who meets a robot on the street.

Research Approach

We draw on records from a commercial robot delivery service, pairing descriptions of what happened during deliveries with the service's own operational records of the same orders. We analyzed this material qualitatively, building a coding scheme over several rounds of independent coding and discussion among researchers.

We also discussed the resulting design directions with a UX practitioner working on the service, to check whether they made sense in practice. Reading the interaction side and the operational side together lets us connect how people behaved around the robot with how deliveries actually turned out.

What We Learned

Delivery is something people accomplish together. Customers responded to robots in varied ways, from curiosity and playfulness to politeness and frustration, and people nearby as well as remote operators often shaped how a delivery unfolded.

Many of the difficulties we saw were not navigation problems but questions of legibility: where to stand, which part of the robot to touch, what the robot is doing at that moment, and what to do when something does not go as expected. Most of these moments were worked out within the encounter itself, frequently with quiet help from operators behind the scenes.

Design Directions

Our findings point toward services that make the robot's current state easy to read at the moment of pickup, physical interfaces that explain themselves, and clear recovery paths for when a pickup does not go as designed. For example, cues on the robot and phone could explain when to wait, when to open the compartment, and how to reach an operator if help is needed. We also propose adapting pickup guidance when a customer's approach or nearby traffic changes the situation. These design directions require further field evaluation.
